When we talk about habit formation from the perspective of neuroscience, we are referring to progressive changes in brain activity that reflect the transition from conscious and deliberate behavior to one that is more automatic and routine. There are several perceptible signs, both external (behavioral) and internal (cognitive or emotional), that indicate that a habit is becoming established:



🟢 1. Reduction of cognitive load
    • What happens: At first, performing the action requires attention and conscious effort. With repetition, the brain begins to “automate” it.
    • How it is perceived:
    • The task is done almost mechanically, without thinking too much about each step.
    • You can talk or think about something else while you do it (for example, brushing your teeth, making coffee, or even writing repetitive code).
    • Neuroscience: The prefrontal cortex (associated with planning and conscious control) is activated less and the basal ganglia is more active, which manages habits and automatic routines.



🟢 2. Association with triggers (cues)
    • What happens: Habits tend to consolidate around specific cues from the environment (time of day, place, object).
    • How it is perceived:
    • Just seeing the stimulus or being in the context automatically triggers the intention to perform the behavior.
    • Example: Seeing the sneakers next to the bed automatically makes you want to go for a run.
    • Neuroscience: The cerebro-striatal pathway is activated, where the brain associates stimulus-action-reward.



🟢 3. Sensation of reward or relief
    • What happens: The brain releases dopamine when the action is executed, reinforcing the stimulus-action connection.
    • How it is perceived:
    • Feeling of satisfaction or “pleasure” after completing the action, even if it is minimal.
    • This occurs even before long-term benefits are noticed; the anticipated reward is enough to reinforce the habit.
    • Neuroscience: Involves the mesolimbic dopaminergic system, especially the nucleus accumbens.



🟢 4. Persistence in the face of distractions or effort
    • What happens: When a habit begins to consolidate, it is carried out even when there are distractions or little conscious motivation.
    • How it is perceived:
    • “I do it without thinking about it, even if I'm tired or busy.”
    • Example: Someone who has become accustomed to meditating in the morning will do so automatically, even if they are sleepy.
    • Neuroscience: The circuitry of the dorsal striatum is strengthened, which maintains automatic action.



🟢 5. Difficulty inhibiting action
    • What happens: The more established the habit is, the more automatic it becomes and the more difficult it is to avoid it.
    • How it is perceived:
    • Trying to stop the behavior creates discomfort or a feeling of “missing something.”
    • Example: Wanting to skip coffee in the morning can generate a feeling of deficit, even though there is no real physiological need.
    • Neuroscience: This relates to the interaction between basal ganglia and prefrontal cortex: automatic action competes with conscious control.



Practical summary

If you observe behavior that:
    1. You do it without thinking too much.
    2. It is triggered by a context or stimulus.
    3. It gives you a small feeling of reward.
    4. Persists even with distractions or lack of motivation.
    5. It's hard to avoid when you want to.

…So, from a neuroscientific point of view, that behavior is in the process of forming a habit.
